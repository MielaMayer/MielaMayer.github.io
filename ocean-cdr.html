Ocean carbon removal · the chemistry, drawn

The ocean keeps a two-column ledger. Every carbon scheme is a bet on one number.

Alkalinity in one column, dissolved carbon in the other. Their ratio decides whether the sea inhales CO₂ or exhales it. Growing plankton, dissolving rock, building calcite shells — each one is just a different way of nudging that ratio. Here is what actually happens, bond by bond.

The rest of this post is just me re-jogging my own memory and trying to break down the current state of Ocean CDR. Posting in case anyone else is doing a similar deep dive and it can be useful. Very subject to mistakes, as it's just me laying out thinking.

Meet the cell — two ledgers at once

A coccolithophore (celebrity species Emiliania huxleyi, “Ehux”) is one cell. Light: photosynthesis = sink into soft tissue (POC). Same hour: calcite plates (coccoliths, PIC) = source at the surface. Climate net is the difference.

Note that: carbon fixed is not carbon removed. A coccolithophore building a beautiful calcite shell is emitting CO₂ to build it. Weathered rock is the only route here that adds alkalinity outright — and alkalinity is the column that does the pulling.

01 / THE ONE IDEA

Two columns, and only their ratio matters

Seawater holds carbon in three chemical costumes at once. Which costume dominates — and therefore whether CO₂ wants to leave the water or enter it — is set by a tug-of-war between two bookkeeping totals.

Column 1 · DIC

Dissolved Inorganic Carbon. Every carbon atom in the water, counted once, no matter its costume:

DIC = [CO₂*] + [HCO₃−] + [CO₃2−]

Push DIC up and the water becomes more likely to exhale CO₂.

Column 2 · Total Alkalinity

Not "how basic the water is." It is a charge count — the excess of proton-acceptors over proton-donors:

TA ≈ [HCO₃−] + 2[CO₃2−] + [B(OH)₄−] + [OH−] − [H+] + …

Push TA up and the water inhales CO₂.

~90%
of surface-ocean DIC is bicarbonate, HCO₃−
Roughly 9% carbonate, and well under 1% actual dissolved CO₂. The ocean is essentially a bicarbonate solution.
~10×
the Revelle buffer penalty
Add 1% to DIC and surface pCO₂ rises ~10%. The ocean resists being loaded — and that same stiffness is why alkalinity is such a powerful lever.
~42×
more carbon in the ocean than the atmosphere
37,200 ± 200 Gt C dissolved versus ~891 Gt C in the air. The ratio was ~62 before industrialisation — the air's share has been growing.
02 / THE CAST

The anions, and which ones the ledger counts

You asked how CO₂ sequestration changes depending on which seawater anion is involved. It comes down to this: an anion contributes to alkalinity in proportion to how many protons it can soak up. A doubly-charged carbonate ion is worth twice a bicarbonate. Sulfate and chloride, which never grab a proton at seawater pH, are worth nothing at all.

The lesson hidden in this grid: an ion's charge is not its carbon value. Sulfate has two negative charges and does nothing. Carbonate has two and does everything. What separates them is proton affinity — the willingness to accept an H⁺ and thereby mop up acidity.
03 / THE LADDER

One carbon atom, three costumes — climbing by shedding protons

Dissolved CO₂ is not stable in seawater. It hydrates, then sheds protons one at a time, climbing a two-rung ladder. Where the population sits on that ladder is set entirely by pH — and pH is set by alkalinity.

04 / THE MASTER MAP

Every carbon scheme, on one diagram

Here is the payoff. Plot any chemical process by what it does to the two columns — how much it changes DIC (across) and how much it changes alkalinity (up). A single diagonal line splits the whole plane into the ocean inhales and the ocean exhales. Nothing else is needed.

●
Read the map once and the rest of this page is commentary. Alkalinity is the vertical axis, and up is the direction of drawdown. Ocean alkalinity enhancement points straight up. Enhanced weathering travels up the diagonal. Growing plankton moves sharply left. Building a shell moves down. That is the whole field.
05 / THE PARADOX

Coccolithophores: the shell is made of carbon and made by emitting carbon

Coccolithophores are single-celled algae roughly five micrometres across that armour themselves in intricate plates of calcite. Blooms of the best-known species — Gephyrocapsa huxleyi, long called Emiliania huxleyi — can cover more than 100,000 km² and turn the sea milky turquoise, plainly visible from orbit. Their fossil plates built the White Cliffs of Dover. And the act of building those plates releases CO₂ into the water.

ΔDIC −1 per CaCO₃
ΔTA −2 per CaCO₃
ΔTA / ΔDIC = 2  →  below the line
Result pCO₂ RISES ↑

Alkalinity falls twice as fast as dissolved carbon. That is the entire mechanism. The carbonate ion the cell consumes was worth two units of alkalinity; the carbon atom it consumes is worth one unit of DIC. Take away twice as much of the pulling column as the pushing column, and the water's grip on CO₂ loosens.

One honest refinement before we go further: not all of that CO₂ escapes. Seawater buffering reabsorbs part of it, so the real figure is about 0.6 moles of CO₂ released per mole of CaCO₃ formed, not 1.0. That number has a name — ψ — and it turns out to be the hinge on which the whole coccolithophore question swings. We come back to it in section 8. It also gets worse as the ocean acidifies: ψ climbs toward 1.0 as CO₂ rises and water cools.

So why is chalk made of dead plankton if they emit carbon?

Because of a timescale switch, and it is the single most-confused point in this whole subject.

  • Over decades to centuries — the timescale a carbon credit is sold on — calcification is a CO₂ source. The released CO₂ is in surface water, in contact with the air, within months.
  • Over tens of thousands to millions of years — the timescale of the White Cliffs of Dover — calcite that reaches the seafloor and is buried removes calcium and carbonate from the ocean permanently, and the geological weathering cycle rebalances the books. On that clock, carbonate burial is a genuine long-term sink.

Both statements are true. They are answers to different questions. A company selling carbon removal is answering the first question, not the second.

The counter-argument: calcite as ballast

There is a real effect pulling the other way, and honest accounting has to include it. Calcite is dense — about 2.7 times the density of seawater. A sinking aggregate of soft organic tissue is barely denser than the water around it and drifts down slowly, giving bacteria weeks to eat it and return its carbon to the dissolved pool. Stud that aggregate with mineral plates and it drops like a stone, outrunning the bacteria and delivering more of its organic carbon to depth. This is the ballast hypothesis, and it is why the relationship between calcifiers and the carbon cycle is genuinely contested rather than simply negative.

06 / THE ROCK ROUTE

Weathering: the planet's own thermostat, run fast

Over geological time, rain that has absorbed CO₂ dissolves silicate rock, and the resulting alkalinity washes to the sea where it holds carbon as bicarbonate. This is the slow feedback that has kept Earth habitable for billions of years. Enhanced weathering is the proposal to run it on a human schedule by grinding the rock up first.

Route A · Silicate weathering — the four-for-one deal

4 CO₂ consumed per Mg₂SiO₄
ΔTA +4  ·  ΔDIC +4
Theoretical max ~1.25 t CO₂ per t olivine
Durability ~10⁵ years as bicarbonate

Route B · Carbonate weathering — calcification, run backwards

Route C · Ocean alkalinity enhancement — skip the rock, add the base

The most direct version of all: manufacture alkalinity and put it in the sea. Slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂, dissolves to give a calcium ion and two hydroxides. Electrochemical approaches split seawater to make a sodium hydroxide stream and a matching acid stream that is disposed of separately. Either way ΔDIC starts at zero and ΔTA jumps — a vector pointing straight up on the master map, the most efficient direction available.

Where it gets hard

  • Grinding costs energy. Dissolution rate scales with surface area, so finer is faster — but milling to fine grades can eat a meaningful fraction of the CO₂ you are trying to capture. The energy budget has to be counted against the removal.
  • Olivine carries passengers. Natural olivine contains nickel and chromium. Spread at scale, those metals go into the coastal environment along with the alkalinity.
  • Push too hard and it snaps back. Add alkalinity too fast or too concentrated and the water becomes supersaturated enough to precipitate CaCO₃ spontaneously — which is calcification, which runs the reaction you just paid for in reverse. This "runaway precipitation" threshold is a hard engineering constraint on how any alkalinity is delivered.
  • The inhale is invisible. Nothing is captured at the moment of release. The CO₂ crosses the sea surface over months, spread across a moving patch of ocean. There is no meter. There is only a model.
07 / THE LIVING ROUTE

Growing plankton: the biggest carbon pump on Earth, and the hardest to sell

Every year, microscopic algae in the sunlit surface ocean fix roughly as much carbon as all land plants combined. A fraction of that sinks out of the surface as dead cells, faecal pellets and aggregates — "marine snow". This is the biological pump, and it is why the deep ocean holds more carbon than the atmosphere.

⚠
The sentence that decides whether any plankton scheme is real: fixing carbon at the surface lowers the DIC of that surface water — but nothing has been removed from the atmosphere until the air refills that deficit across the sea surface. That exchange takes months to a year. If the water sinks, mixes away, or the carbon is eaten and released before the inhale completes, the paper removal never happened.

Depth is durability

Carbon that sinks is not gone; it is only away, and for a length of time set almost entirely by how deep it got before something ate it. Sinking flux falls off sharply with depth — the Martin curve — because bacteria and zooplankton intercept most of the falling material in the first few hundred metres and respire its carbon straight back into dissolved form.

The cautionary history: iron fertilisation

The idea of stimulating plankton to draw down carbon is not new. Thirteen major open-ocean iron fertilisation experiments were run between 1993 and 2009. They reliably produced blooms. What they did not reliably produce was measurable, durable carbon export — and the field's own verdict became markedly more cautious as a result.

  • Export was small and wildly variable. Big blooms often produced little sinking flux; most of the fixed carbon was recycled in the surface layer rather than exported.
  • Nutrient robbing. Nutrients consumed in one patch are nutrients unavailable downstream. Some of the apparent local gain is productivity stolen from elsewhere.
  • Side effects at depth. Respiring a large pulse of organic matter consumes oxygen and can generate nitrous oxide and methane — greenhouse gases far more potent than CO₂ per molecule.
  • Verification proved brutally hard. Attributing a change in a moving, mixing, patchy ocean to your intervention rather than to weather is the central unsolved problem, and it is unsolved for every ocean-biology approach, not just iron.
08 / THE DECIDING NUMBER

The rain ratio: how much shell per unit of flesh

Sections 5 and 7 described two pumps running in the same water with opposite signs. Which one wins is settled by a single ratio: how much mineral carbon (PIC) a plankton community exports per unit of organic carbon (POC). This is the rain ratio, and the threshold is not where most people guess.

Where the number 1.65 comes from

Not all the CO₂ released by calcification escapes — seawater's buffering absorbs some of it. Frankignoulle and colleagues (1994) named that fraction ψ: the moles of CO₂ actually released per mole of CaCO₃ precipitated.

ψ ≈ 0.6 mol CO₂ per mol CaCO₃
threshold = 1 / ψ
≈ 1.65 PIC : POC

One unit of organic carbon fixed removes one unit of DIC. One unit of calcite formed releases only 0.6. So it takes about 1.65 units of calcite to cancel one unit of soft tissue — not 1.0, which is the shorthand you will often see and which is off by about 65%.

⚠
Both popular claims are wrong. "Coccolithophores lock carbon away in their shells" is wrong — the shell-building step is a CO₂ source. But "coccolithophores are CO₂ emitters" is an over-correction — the same cell photosynthesises, and for most species the organic half wins. The honest statement is narrower and more useful: calcification is a carbon cost that the organism's photosynthesis has to pay for, and how much is left over depends on the species, the season and the strain.

Three further complications keep this from being a clean calculation:

  • The threshold itself is moving. ψ climbs toward 1.0 as water cools and as CO₂ rises — so the tipping point falls as the ocean acidifies. The penalty on calcification gets worse over time, not better.
  • Ballast cuts the other way. Klaas and Archer (2002), standardising sediment-trap data from 52 sites below 1,000 m, found mineral ballast explains 85–90% of the variance in deep organic carbon flux, with calcite the dominant carrier — about 0.072 g of organic carbon delivered per gram of CaCO₃. A calcifier may cost you carbon at the surface and still deliver more of it to depth.
  • Acidification is reshaping the organisms. Riebesell et al. (2000) found reduced calcification under high CO₂; Iglesias-Rodriguez et al. (2008) found the opposite. Meyer and Riebesell's 2015 meta-analysis of 23 studies and 48 experiments came down on the side of reduced calcification in the two dominant species — but with four qualitatively different response shapes across 14 strains. No single sentence about "how coccolithophores respond" survives contact with the data.

10 / THE HARD PART

None of this is the hard part. Proving it happened is.

Everything above is textbook chemistry. It is not in dispute. The reason marine carbon removal is difficult has almost nothing to do with the reactions and almost everything to do with the fact that you cannot put a meter on the ocean. The removal happens as a diffuse, months-long inhale across a moving patch of sea. There is no flue to measure. There is only a model with a measured input.

⚠
Sit with the first two numbers together. Roughly 600,000 tonnes contracted against roughly 730 tonnes independently verified. That is not an accusation against anyone in particular — it is simply what a market of forward promises looks like before the verification catches up.
